# Get Out (2017)

## Overview
Get Out is a 2017 American horror film written and directed by Jordan Peele in his feature directorial debut. The film stars Daniel Kaluuya as Chris Washington, a Black photographer who visits his white girlfriend Rose's (Allison Williams) family estate and discovers a nightmarish truth. Get Out was produced on a $4.5 million budget and grossed over $255 million worldwide. It won the Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay and was universally praised as one of the most inventive horror films of its era.

## Plot
Chris Washington (Daniel Kaluuya) travels with his white girlfriend Rose to meet her parents at their rural estate. Rose's family — father Dean (Bradley Whitford), mother Missy (Catherine Keener), and brother Jeremy (Caleb Landry Jones) — are relentlessly, uncomfortably enthusiastic about Chris. The family's Black employees and groundskeepers behave strangely — speaking with unnatural cadences, their eyes occasionally rolling back.

The film's central horror is gradually revealed: Rose's family has developed a procedure to transplant white consciousnesses into Black bodies, discarding the original person into a mental prison called "the Sunken Place." The Armitage family hosts annual parties at which wealthy white buyers bid on Black candidates. Rose's seduction of Chris was a trap.

The film's climax is a kinetic escape sequence that subverts the typical horror film logic in which the Black character dies first.

## Themes
- **Racial appropriation and liberal racism**: The horror of a white progressive community that admires Black people while treating them as objects for consumption
- **The Sunken Place**: A powerful metaphor for the alienation of consciousness — being a spectator in your own body, your thoughts and reactions trapped behind glass
- **Black bodies as commodity**: The film literalises how white culture appropriates and exploits Black physicality and talent
- **The social horror of micro-aggressions**: The early scenes build dread from recognisable racial awkwardness
- **Genre as political vehicle**: Peele uses horror conventions to make an argument about race in America that would be impossible in a realist mode
